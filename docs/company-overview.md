# PART 1 — BUSINESS

## 1.1 Positioning

### One line

> A custom software development agency that builds, finishes, or fixes software. Fixed price agreed up front, working software every week, and you own all of it.

### The structure: generalist brand, specific pages

**At brand level: a generalist custom software development agency.** No narrow niche is claimed in the hero, the page title, or the metadata.

**At page level: specific.** Each service page targets one problem, and outbound campaigns point at those pages, never at the homepage.

This layering *is* the position. Both halves are load-bearing:

- A generalist brand does not starve the pipeline while there is no referral network.
- Specific pages are what make cold outreach and search work at all. A generic page converts nobody.

**The risk:** this only holds while the pages stay genuinely specific. If they drift toward "we do everything well", the result is the downside of generalism with none of the focus.

**It is also a holding position, not the end state.** After three to five delivered projects there will be evidence of what the company is actually good at and who pays well for it. That is when to specialise properly. The architecture was chosen so that is cheap: changing the sharp end means changing a hero and a funnel page, not repositioning the company.

### What it used to be, and why it changed

The previous position was **"AI-native MVP builder for SaaS founders."** The market research behind it was kept and is still valid; it is now one funnel rather than the identity.

**1. It targeted the buyer most able to replace us with tooling.** AI cuts routine coding time roughly 46% (McKinsey, Feb 2026). Junior developer demand is down about 40% where AI tooling is seriously deployed. A technically capable SaaS founder can now self-serve a first version and increasingly treats the work as commodity-priced.

**2. Claiming a specialism with zero proof produces a confused website, not a position.** The specialisation literature: *"premature specialization kills more software agencies than staying generalist too long ever has"*, and *"if you have one case study in your chosen niche, you're not ready... you need at least three to five real proof points to be credible."* Its named failure mode is *"announcing the new positioning but not changing anything else. The website still lists ten services."* That described this site exactly.

**3. The audiences arrive through different channels.** Early-stage founders are advised to "ask five trusted founders for referrals rather than Google", so they arrive already introduced and use the site as a credibility check. Businesses buying a CRM do Google, and land on a specific page. One narrow homepage serves neither.

### Where demand is growing instead

**Rescue and rebuild.** This evidence is stronger than an earlier version of this document allowed, and it is **safe to cite**. It was previously hedged alongside the banned vendor figures, which left the best available ammunition unused.

- **GitClear**, analysing **211 million changed lines** across open-source and private repositories: technical debt rises **30 to 41%** after AI tool adoption, duplicated code blocks up **8x during 2024**, refactoring down from **25% of changed lines in 2021 to under 10% in 2024**, copy/paste at **15.7% in H1 2026**. Covered by **LeadDev**. This is independent research with a legible method, not vendor content.
- **Stack Overflow developer survey**, **49,000 respondents across 177 countries**: **84% use AI tools**, **29% trust them, down 11 points year on year**, only **3% "highly trust"**, and **46% actively distrust** the output. Experienced developers are the most sceptical.
- **The single best line for sales, and it is the buyer's own words: 66% of developers say their biggest problem is "AI solutions that are almost right, but not quite."** That is the rescue pitch, from a citable survey. It is on the rescue page, attributed.
- Independently, demand for senior engineering judgment (integration complexity, data quality, AI governance, production stability) is described as growing rather than shrinking.

*(An earlier version of this document said trust "fell from 43% to 29%". The actual finding is 29%, down 11 points. Corrected, because an overstated figure in a document whose entire thesis is sourcing discipline is the wrong error to leave standing.)*

**Non-technical SMBs.** They cannot self-serve at all, and their spend is shifting "aggressively toward Business Process Re-engineering" rather than simple implementation.

### CRITICAL SOURCING RULE

**The figures "8,000 startups needed rescue engineering by mid-2026" and "the $4 billion technical debt crisis" come from vendor blogs selling rescue services. Self-interested, never independently verified. DO NOT REUSE THEM ANYWHERE, including sales calls.**

What is credible is the *direction*, because three independent angles agree: the pull-request study, the trust-decline survey, and the senior-demand observation. **Sell the direction, never the numbers.**

Same skepticism applies to competitor pricing. A dedicated research pass mostly failed to verify competitor rate cards. One claim was actively **refuted**: a "$60K to $180K+" figure attributed to the agency Simform traced to an unverifiable third-party blog, and Simform publishes no rates. Do not reuse it or anything sourced that way.

### Verified market demand (safe to cite)

- **Australia:** A$5.4B raised in 2025, up 31% year on year, third-largest year on record. A$1.8B in Q1 2026 alone, up 63%.
- **US:** venture funding hit $412.7B in H1 2026; AI companies took $355.9B of that, 86%.
- **UK:** £17.5bn across 2,000+ deals in 2025, AI capturing over £6bn.
- **Capital is concentrating in AI-enabled and vertical software**, not generic horizontal SaaS. In Australia specifically, vertical business software took 80% of software capital against 20% horizontal.
- **UAE leads globally in generative AI adoption**, 64% of working-age population by end of 2025 against 26% in Saudi Arabia. This is the strongest actionable signal for the Gulf.
- **TypeScript is now the #1 language on GitHub**, overtaking Python, explicitly AI-driven because static typing is a safety net for AI-generated code. This validates the existing stack as a current advantage, not just a preference.
- **1.1M+ public GitHub repos now use LLM SDKs**, up 178% year on year.

**Important nuance, do not oversell:** AI's funding share is driven overwhelmingly by mega-rounds into a handful of frontier labs, not broad early-stage funding. Seed and angel funding actually *fell* 15% quarter on quarter in Q2 2026 even as headline totals hit records. **Translation: the target client's own funding pool is a shrinking slice of a growing headline number.** Use "AI is where investor attention is", never "AI funding is exploding for seed-stage founders."

**Do not over-index on agentic AI.** 52% of developers do not use AI agents at all, 38% have no plans to, and only 33% trust AI output accuracy. It is the buzzword everyone is chasing but adoption is still early.

**Gulf venture funding figures are publicly unreliable.** Most specific dollar amounts and growth-rate claims for Saudi and UAE failed verification, with sources contradicting each other. Lead with the UAE adoption stat instead.

## 1.2 What must NEVER be claimed

Absolute. Violations have been found and removed from this site more than once.

- **No fabricated statistics.** No client counts, satisfaction rates, retention rates, or "projects delivered" counters. Real removals from this site: "200+ companies", "Join 500+ operators", "60% manual time reduction", "30-40% cloud bill savings", and a client retention rate.
- **No client logos**, and no placeholder logos presented as clients.
- **No case studies.** There are none.
- **GMS, Hexadesk and Healthline are NOT live and must never be cited as shipped proof points.** GMS and Hexadesk were abandoned by the client; Healthline is confirmed not live. Older strategy notes cited them; that guidance is dead.
- **No prices anywhere public**, including metadata shown in search results.
- **No implied track record on any page.** Every page says what the company *would* do. That is a process statement and is allowed. Nothing says what it *has* done.
- **Never criticise other developers or agencies**, especially on rescue pages. The reader may have written the code.
- **Never imply ERP product replacement.** Not an off-the-shelf ERP vendor, not a migration shop. Never suggest replacing or migrating anyone off SAP, NetSuite or Odoo.
- **No payroll processing.** Regulated, deliberately out of scope. HR means records, leave and approvals only.
- **No SEO ranking guarantees.**
- **The company does not run ads or manage campaigns.** It builds software for marketing teams. That distinction is why service 9 exists in its current form.
- **No vertical lock** (healthtech, legaltech, fintech specialisation) until two or three clients close and a pattern emerges naturally.

## 1.3 The five client-acquisition segments

Ranked by realistic near-term fit.

**1. Bootstrapped and self-funded founders.** Untouched by venture cycles, invisible in funding databases, budget-disciplined, strong fit for fixed price. Likely the largest realistic near-term pool.

**2. Existing software companies retrofitting AI.** A large backlog of companies with live products and no AI features. Sell as a standalone entry point (a production AI feature added to a live product), not only as a post-MVP upsell.

**3. Inherited and unfinished work.** Someone arrives with a codebase another team built, or that they built themselves with AI tooling, and it no longer works predictably. Distinct from every other segment and plausibly the easiest to close with no case studies: budget already proven, need already validated, and real urgency, because their alternative is starting over. Served by the Code Rescue page and sold via the fixed-price review.

**4. Non-venture SMBs and traditional businesses.** Clinics, law firms, agencies wanting internal tools or customer-facing features. Least glamorous, most insulated from AI-hype cycles, and they actually use Google. Served by the CRM, ERP, analytics and marketing-tooling pages.

**5. Funded pre-seed and seed vertical SaaS founders. Demoted from third to last.** The original research segment, and still real, but the funding pool is contracting in a way earlier versions of this document treated as a footnote. **Carta data: seed rounds down 28% in count and 37% in dollars, pre-seed instruments down 13%, later-stage now about 47% of all capital.** A K-shaped market where mega-rounds lift headline totals while the number of early-stage cheques falls. That is a structural headwind, not a nuance, and it is why segments 3 and 4 were promoted above it.

**Messaging must differ per segment.** Do not force AI-native framing onto a bootstrapped founder whose product does not need it. A plain fixed-price build with no AI is a legitimate offer.

### Ideal client profile

- **Stage:** pre-seed to seed (roughly $250K to $2M raised) **or** bootstrapped with a comparable ceiling. Funding status is not a qualifier; budget is.
- **Team:** solo or small founding team, usually no in-house engineering. **This describes the MVP buyer, not every service's buyer.** Companies buying DevOps or automation work often already have engineers, and that is exactly who buys it.
- **Budget:** realistically $2.5K to $25K for a first build.

## 1.4 Offers and pricing

| Package | Scope | Timeline |
|---|---|---|
| **Code Review and Rescue Assessment** | Read an existing codebase and infrastructure, produce a written assessment: what works, what is risky, what is missing. No code changes. Rebuild or completion quoted separately afterwards. | Under a week, fixed price |
| **Scoping Sprint** | For an idea not yet built. Work out what it actually takes and write it down: the screens, the data, what comes first. Produces a spec and a fixed quote for the build. | Short, fixed price |
| **MVP Sprint** | One core user journey, authentication, basic dashboard. No AI feature. | 4 to 6 weeks |
| **AI-Native MVP** | Full MVP plus one production AI feature (chatbot, RAG or automation), Stripe billing, multi-tenant ready. | 6 to 10 weeks |
| **Scale Retainer** | Post-launch: feature sprints, DevOps and monitoring, analytics, ongoing AI iteration. | Monthly, ongoing |

Add-ons sellable into any tier: SEO and performance pass, growth funnel setup, business process automation. On request, not headline services.

**Two entry offers, one for each starting point.** The Code Review is the way in for software that already exists. The **Scoping Sprint is its mirror for people starting from nothing**, who would otherwise jump straight from a free call to committing to a full build. Both are small, fixed price, and leave the client holding something they keep whether they continue or not.

### Why the Code Review exists, and why its structure matters

**Rescue work is unscoped by nature, and quoting a fixed price on a codebase nobody has read is how agencies lose money.** Pricing the review separately is what makes fixed pricing safe here: the rebuild is quoted only after the code has been read.

It is also the cheapest route out of the proof-point problem. Reviews are small and fast, so they produce delivered engagements much quicker than MVP builds, and those become the three to five proof points needed before a specialism can be claimed.

**The client keeps the written assessment whether or not they continue.** This is a structural guarantee and the most important trust asset the company has. Conversion research is direct: *"a structural guarantee that lets clients walk away without loss does more trust-building than any case study."*

### PRICING POLICY: NO PRICES ON THE WEBSITE

**Absolute. No figure, no range, no "starting at", anywhere public, including metadata shown in search results.**

This was tried during launch and reversed. A visible starting price is easy to screenshot and compare against a cheaper competitor with no context on what is included, so it invites price shopping instead of qualifying leads.

**Price is quoted verbally on the free discovery call**, after hearing real scope. That still filters budget mismatches, through conversation instead of a number.

**Packages, timelines and inclusions ARE public.** Only the money is held back.

### Internal pricing anchors — NOT FOR THE WEBSITE, NOT FOR CLIENTS

| Offer | Anchor |
|---|---|
| **Code Review** | $900 to $1,500 fixed |
| **Scoping Sprint** | $1,000 to $2,000 fixed |
| **MVP Sprint** | from $8,000 |
| **AI-Native MVP** | from $15,000 |
| **Rescue rebuild** | quoted after the review. Benchmark: a regulated-industry specialist charges $15,000 to $40,000 |
| **Scale Retainer** | $2,500 to $6,000/month. Not independently verified, inferred from offshore norms |

### The unit economics that decided this

Earlier anchors were **$2,500** and **$7,000**. They were replaced because **$2,500 does not cover delivery.** At **PKR 200,000/month ≈ $720** for one experienced contract developer (rate 277.5, August 2026):

| | |
|---|---|
| One senior dev, 5 weeks (1.15 months) | $830 |
| Realistically 1.5 heads (dev plus part design/PM) | ~$1,250 |
| At 65% utilisation, covering sales, proposals, admin and gaps between projects | ~$1,915 |
| Plus fixed-price overrun allowance, ~10% blended | ~$2,100 |
| Plus overhead: tooling, cloud, accounting, equipment | **~$2,350** |

**At $2,500, contribution is roughly $150.** That is not a margin, and it assumes nothing goes wrong on a fixed-price engagement, which is the one thing they reliably do. At $8,000 the same delivery is about **70% gross margin**.

For market context: published 2026 ranges put MVPs at **$15,000 to $60,000** average, lean single-workflow builds at **$8,000 to $25,000**, and offshore mid-complexity at **$10,000 to $35,000**. **$2,500 was below the floor of every published range.**

### Why the old reasoning was wrong, recorded so it is not reintroduced

The previous justification was: *"a new shop with no public case studies asking $10K+ upfront is asking for trust it has not earned."* That is wrong twice over.

1. **Price is a quality signal.** With no case studies to evaluate, a buyer uses price as the proxy. Cheap and unproven does not read as good value, it reads as high risk. Expensive and unproven at least reads as conviction.
2. **Zero margin prevents ever earning the proof.** At $150 contribution you cannot afford to over-deliver, so the first projects get rushed, so they never become case studies worth showing, so prices never rise. **Underpricing blocks the exact thing that would let you stop underpricing.** This is the trap, and it is the real argument.

Also: **there is always someone cheaper.** A solo freelancer with no overhead will undercut any floor price indefinitely. Competing there means competing against a cost base we cannot match, which is the race to the bottom this strategy already rejected.

**It is worst for rescue**, which is the *least* price-sensitive segment, because the buyer's alternative is starting over. Anchoring near $2,500 while a specialist charges $15,000 to $40,000 for the same work signals not understanding the problem. On a trust-sensitive rebuild, cheap is disqualifying.

### The lever for having no proof is entry size, not price

**What de-risks a buyer with no case studies to check is a small first step, not a cheap whole project.** That is what the Code Review and the Scoping Sprint are for: both are small, both are fixed price, and both leave the client with something they keep even if they walk away.

That converts "discount to buy trust" into "get paid to earn it", and it produces delivered engagements far faster than full builds, which is how the first real proof points get made.

**The arbitrage argument, verifiable and safe in conversation:** US developer costs benchmark at $110K to $180K+ per year. Even at $15,000, an AI-native MVP is a fraction of a few weeks of one US senior engineer fully loaded.

**Do not price against a competitor rate card.** One does not reliably exist publicly. Price against these unit economics.

# PART 2 — GO TO MARKET AND FUNNEL

## 2.1 Channel ranking

By conversion quality, based on verified data:

1. **Referral and partnership.** Converts up to **19x higher**, with 74% shorter sales cycles and 73% lower customer acquisition cost than cold outreach. Partner with pre-seed accelerators, angel syndicates, fractional CTOs, design shops.
2. **Personalised LinkedIn outbound.** 15 to 30% response rates against generic. Omnichannel (LinkedIn plus email) lifts purchase intent 287%.
3. **Niche communities.** Five to eight small active founder or vertical groups outperform large generic ones.
4. **Content and SEO.** Cheap and durable but slow to compound.
5. **Paid ads.** Lowest priority. No reliable cost-per-lead benchmark exists for this profile. Do not lead spend here.

## 2.2 The channel actually being worked first: rescue-led cold outbound

The ranking above is by conversion *quality*, not by what is available from a standing start. Referral converts best but needs a network that does not exist yet. Content is fourth and will not produce a client this quarter. **Outbound is the only channel where volume is controllable from zero.**

**The real argument is the feedback loop. Reply rate answers "does rescue resonate" in about two weeks. SEO would take six months to answer the same question.** Test positioning where the answer arrives fast, then let what works inform the slower channels.

With no case studies, *"agencies winning new business through cold email treat their own outbound as the best case study they have."*

### Infrastructure, before a single email

- **DO NOT send cold email from alphabrackets.com.** Cold volume damages domain reputation, and that domain carries info@alphabrackets.com, currently the only working inbound channel on the site. Use a separate sending domain pointing at the same site.
- **SPF, DKIM and DMARC on the sending domain before the first send.** The research is blunt that the failures here are technical, not copywriting.
- **Warm the domain.** Start at 20 to 30 emails per inbox per day, increase as reputation builds, never spike.
- **The booking link must be live first.** Sending outbound to a site whose only action is a mailto link wastes the sends.

### Target signals, ranked by how loudly the prospect has already declared the problem

**These are untested hypotheses, not verified channels.**

1. **Job ads seeking a developer to "take over", "maintain an existing", or "inherit" a codebase.** Strongest available: publicly stated, dated, names the exact problem, and implies budget.
2. **Founders posting publicly about being stuck** with a codebase or an AI-built app (Indie Hackers, Reddit, X). Declared problem, but approach with help rather than a pitch.
3. **App store reviews on small apps complaining of crashes and bugs.** Specific and quotable.
4. **Launch directories 12 to 24 months old** where the product is still up but has not moved. Weaker, more inference.
5. **Sites failing performance and Core Web Vitals checks.** Feeds the SEO and performance service rather than rescue.

**Start with 1 and 2 only.** If those produce no replies, either the message or the segment is wrong, and that is worth knowing before building lists at scale.

### Message shape

Four beats:

1. **A specific observation about them.** The job ad, the review, the post. **If the opening sentence could be sent to anyone, the email has failed.**
2. **What usually causes that**, stated as expertise rather than diagnosis. Credibility without claiming to know their codebase.
3. **The low-risk offer:** a fixed-price review, and they keep the written assessment whether or not they continue.
4. **Ask for a conversation, not a sale.** The goal of a cold email is to start a conversation.

**Never criticise whoever built the thing.** The reader may be that person.

### Reading the results

- **Replies at all** → the segment is real, iterate the message.
- **Opens but no replies** → message problem, not segment problem.
- **Nothing** → change signal or segment before writing more pages.

Reply rate is the metric, not opens.

## 2.4 Booking questions (Cal.com)

Five custom fields. Cal.com already captures name and email, so the default "Additional notes" field should be turned off because question 1 replaces it.

1. **What are you building?** Long text, required.
2. **Where is it right now?** Select, required: just an idea / designs done, nothing built / part-built, in development / built but not launched / live with users / **another team built it and I need someone to take it over**.
3. **What is driving the timing?** Select, required: investor demo or raise / a customer is waiting / a launch date we set / no fixed date.
4. **Have you set a budget for this?** Select, required: yes, approved / rough figure in mind / not yet, want to understand cost first.
5. **How did you hear about us?** Select, optional: referral / LinkedIn / Google / a community / other.

Three deliberate choices:

- **Question 4 is not a price-bracket dropdown.** Brackets on a booking screen are screenshot-able and anchor the client before scope is heard, which is the exact failure the no-public-pricing rule prevents. This filters no-budget enquiries without publishing a figure.
- **Question 5 is the only attribution that exists** while on-site analytics is off, and four channels are being tested at once. It is load-bearing.
- **Nothing asks about AI.** "Do you need an AI feature?" invites a yes from people who do not, and contradicts the published FAQ answer that says the company will tell someone when they do not need one. Question 1 surfaces it honestly.

Question 2's last option is the cheapest available test of whether the inherited-work segment is real.

### Booking setup facts

- **The public address is info@alphabrackets.com.** It is a **Hostinger alias, not a mailbox with its own calendar.** Mail does arrive, so the 48-hour reply promise is keepable, but there is no calendar behind the address.
- Cal.com cannot connect a calendar to it directly (its integrations are Google, Outlook/365, Zoho and CalDAV; Hostinger mail is none of those). Use info@alphabrackets.com as the Cal.com **account email** and connect a separate Google or Outlook calendar for availability.
- **Without a connected calendar, double-booking is the default, not a risk.**
- **The event must be 30 minutes and free.** The site already says both in several places. A 15 or 45 minute event, or one with a price, makes published copy untrue.
- Display name "Alpha Brackets". Claim the `alphabrackets` username so the URL stays clean.
- **Verified working on 20 Aug 2026.** A booking was made successfully against `https://cal.com/alphabrackets/30min`. One thing still worth a look if it has not been checked: Cal.com can surface the *connected calendar's* address as the organiser rather than the account address, so confirm the confirmation email and calendar entry show "Alpha Brackets" and not a personal address. If a personal address leaks, the fix is Google Workspace on the domain.

## 2.5 Analytics: deliberately off

There is no on-site analytics. Search Console plus Cal.com's own booking record answers the only two questions that matter at this stage: is anyone finding the site, and is anyone booking. Adding a script now means a cookie-consent obligation and a privacy-policy expansion in exchange for measuring traffic that does not exist yet.

Revisit when Search Console shows real impressions. Then prefer a cookieless tool (Plausible, Umami, or Vercel Analytics) over restoring the old CMS tracker, which reached the CMS by request header and is what forced the whole site off static rendering.

## 2.6 SEO reality check

**For a brand-new zero-authority domain, generic head terms are effectively unrankable.** "MVP development company", "AI development company", "custom software development company" all have real competition from established agencies with years of backlinks. Use them for on-page relevance, not as ranking targets. Corroborated across five independent sources.

**Target keyword difficulty under about 30** as a practical new-site ceiling, but treat it as a heuristic. Keyword difficulty is purely a referring-domain-count model and explicitly does not account for content quality or relevance. Always check the actual results page manually before committing to a term.

**A lower-volume high-intent keyword beats a higher-volume low-intent one.** The consensus example: 200 searches a month with strong commercial intent outperforms 5,000 to 12,000 with weak intent, because generic informational traffic does not convert.

**Long-tail queries are the realistic near-term win.** Three-plus-word queries are the large majority of all queries by count.

### No verified volume data exists yet

**Two research passes have failed to obtain tool-verified search volume.** This is an open task, and it is a manual one:

- **Google Trends cannot be queried programmatically.** No public API, it deflects datacenter traffic (returns `429`, which is bot deflection, not a quota), and it is a JavaScript app whose served HTML contains no data. Not a retry-and-hope problem.
- **Trends would not answer the question anyway.** It reports *relative* interest on a 0 to 100 index, not searches per month. Sizing a page needs absolute volume and difficulty, which Trends structurally does not provide.
- **Ahrefs, Semrush and Keyword Planner all need a paid subscription or a live Google Ads account.**

**What works:** Google Keyword Planner (free with a Google Ads account, no spend needed, gives volume ranges) · Google Trends **in a browser** (works fine, exports CSV) · Ahrefs Free Keyword Generator or Ubersuggest free tier · and once verified, **Search Console**, which gives real impressions rather than estimates.

**This is roughly a 30 minute human task and cannot be automated.**

### Keyword targets (volume unverified)

**Homepage and brand:** custom software development company · code rescue service · take over unfinished software project · MVP development company for startups · SaaS MVP development agency · AI integration for SaaS startups · custom CRM development.

**Rescue page:** code rescue service · rebuild AI generated code · fix vibe coded app · take over unfinished software project · software audit service · inherited codebase developer. Long-tail worth checking first: "what to do with an unfinished software project", "developer to take over existing project", "is my AI generated code production ready", "how to audit an inherited codebase".

**Blog and informational, best near-term opportunity:** how much does an MVP cost · MVP development cost for startups · hire MVP developer · MVP development for bootstrapped SaaS founders · how to add AI features to existing SaaS product.

**Avoid:** vertical-locked terms ("healthtech MVP development", "legaltech AI agency"). No vertical is chosen, so the content investment is unjustified.

## 3.5 Anatomy of a service page

Every service page renders, in order:

1. **Hero** — title, description, background image.
2. **Intro** — the card intro as a heading, plus a "Solutions We Provide" checklist.
3. **Offer** *(only where the service defines one; currently only Code Rescue)* — a named, buyable first step with what is included, the guarantee, and what happens next. Sits **above** the process, because someone already convinced should not scroll past five steps to find what they can buy.
4. **Process timeline** — four or five named steps with descriptions.

### The rule about statistics on service pages

**A service gets a stat only when there is one that is both true and about that service.** There is deliberately **no default**.

This exists because of a real bug: a default stats list plus a top-up mechanism meant all ten service pages showed the same two numbers, and the timeline appeared twice on every page. Worse, the claims were wrong for most services: "Weeks to MVP" appeared on the SEO page, which is not an MVP build, and "10x Load Without A Rewrite" on UI/UX Design, which has no load story.

**The test for adding one:** is it a commitment we set (a timeline), or a factual property of the work (one codebase, two platforms)? Those are fine. **A client outcome is not**, until there are real clients to measure. This is why "60% manual time reduction" and "30-40% cloud bill savings" were deleted.

**Five of thirteen services have a stat. Eight have none, and render no stat pills rather than borrowing someone else's.**

## 7.1 Stack

## 7.2 Project structure

- `src/app/` — pages and routes, plus the generated icon, social image, robots and sitemap.
- `src/components/sections/` — page-specific sections, grouped by page.
- `src/components/shared/` — components reused across pages.

- `src/types/` — TypeScript types. The CMS types file is auto-generated and must not be hand-edited.
- `docs/` — this document, plus strategy, project notes, SEO research and the internal cost calculation.
- `public/brand/` — brand identity assets, kept separate from decoration.
- `public/images/backgrounds/` — five section background photos.
- `public/images/patterns/` — decorative overlays.

**Asset discipline:** the old asset tree held 35MB, of which **190 files were unused template leftovers**. It is now 1.2MB. **Before adding an image, check it will actually render.** Roughly half the files under `src/` are likewise unreferenced template code, so do not assume a file is live just because it exists.

## 7.3 Known gotchas

- **The build cache goes stale after moving files under `public/`.** The dev server 404s on assets that moved while the source is already correct. This looks exactly like a broken reference and will send you hunting through components for nothing. **Delete the `.next` directory and restart before believing an asset 404.** To tell them apart: the production output directory is rebuilt from current source, the dev cache is not. If only the dev cache has the old path, it is the cache.
- **The TypeScript build info file can go stale.** If type checking seems to miss real errors, delete it and rerun.
- **CMS fetches can fail locally**, returning an authentication error. Several pages had their fetches replaced with empty typed arrays so the site renders without live CMS content.
- **Five lint errors are pre-existing baseline**, all "unexpected any" in the CMS client. A change should add none. Any other count means something new was introduced.
- **Windows path quirks:** a bare `/` argument to curl can get mangled by path conversion in Git Bash.
- **Grepping built HTML double-counts.** Rendered markup and the React payload both contain the text, so a naive grep inflates counts. **Scope checks to the rendered body**, cutting the string before the payload marker. A specific trap: grepping for a dollar sign followed by a digit returns dozens of false hits from payload markers. It looks like leaked prices and is not.

## 7.4 Enforced-by-compiler rules

Two things are structurally impossible rather than just documented:

- **Every service must declare a category.** The field is required, not optional, so a service without one fails to compile rather than silently vanishing from the catalog page.
- **Every category must be listed in the render order array.** There is a compile-time exhaustiveness check. Adding a category without listing it would hide every service in it with no error anywhere, which is the one failure mode of grouping that is invisible in review. **This check depends on how the array is declared; a plain type annotation silently disables it.**

## 7.5 Conventions

- **Disabled routes and sections stay in the code, commented out, never deleted.** They are off because there is no content, not because they are gone. Keep this pattern when disabling anything new.
- **One primary CTA per page.** The global closing CTA already exists on every page except the FAQ page, so a second one on the same page is a duplicate. This has been corrected more than once.
- **Every email address lives in one config file and nowhere else**, so changing the public address cannot leave a stale copy behind.
- **Icons are imported through a single abstraction file**, never directly from the icon library, so the library can be swapped in one place.

## 8.4 Recurring failure modes to watch for

Each of these has actually happened on this project, more than once in some cases:

- **Fabricated statistics creeping back in.** Five separate instances have been found and removed.
- **A default value creating false uniformity.** A default stats list made all ten service pages claim identical numbers. Absence must mean absence.
- **Duplicate CTAs**, because the global closing CTA is easy to forget when adding a section.
- **Copying a large agency's site structure.** Two thirds of it exists to display proof this company does not have.
- **Reading a strategy document sentence as more prescriptive than it is.** Two separate errors came from treating a segment description or the ideal-client profile as an eligibility rule for every service.
- **Confusing honesty with underselling.** The ERP page narrowed its own scope trying to be honest, and undersold real capability. Not claiming a track record is honesty. Shrinking the work you can do is not.
- **Trusting a naive grep of built HTML.** It double-counts, and the dollar-sign check produces alarming false positives.
- **Assuming an asset 404 is a code bug.** It is usually the stale build cache.
